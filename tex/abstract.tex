\begin{abstract}
Complex multicellularity exemplifies the surprising constructive power of evolution.
% morphological and physiological
% successive
While natural history has produced extensive innovation in form and function along some multicell lineages, evolutionary processes underlying this complexity remain opaque.
As a general, tractable model, we investigated this issue using digital multicells: assemblages of cell-like computer programs that collectively replicate, mutate, and compete.
% In evolutionary trials where digital organisms could form group structures akin to multicellular bodies, fitness advantage arose from neither quantity of adaptive genome content nor quantity of adaptive phenotypic traits.
% Long-standing curiosity, in particular, persists as to how adaptive selection and neutral ratcheting interact to produce complexity in these lineages.
Among evolutionary outcomes, we observed an extreme outlier in complexity measures with patterned multicellular growth and multi-stage life history.
% Across replicates, we observed
Case study analysis of this lineage revealed biogenic niche construction, which imposed crowding interference on alternate morphologies.
Neutralizing biogenic context in replay experiments revealed that this case study lineage, in fact, exerted biotic selection to maintain its own complexity.
% Analogous scenarios arose across replicates
Such ecological lock-in of complexity appears across replicates, with correlation between niche construction and functional complexity matching or exceeding the effects of adaptation and drift.
These findings show how biotic selection can act as a powerful complexity ratchet, and concretely demonstrate a possibly critical role of eco-evolutionary feedback in early evolution of biological multicellularity.
\end{abstract}
