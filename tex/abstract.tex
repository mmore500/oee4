\begin{abstract}
Complex multicellularity is foundational to macroscopic life, yet explanations for its emergence remain hotly contested and difficult to test experimentally.
In particular, it is disputed whether expansion of functional genome content in multicell lineages is driven primarily by positive selection for conferred adaptive benefits or, instead, arose through drift accumulation of arbitrary contingencies.

We examined this issue using a model system of digital organisms: computer programs that evolve as they replicate, mutate, and compete.
Across evolutionary trials allowing these digital organisms to form group structures akin to multicellular bodies, high-complexity lineages exhibited no inherent fitness advantage --- consistent with neutralist expectations.
However, closer analysis of a high-complexity lineage exhibiting patterned growth revealed a different evolutionary driver.
This lineage outcompeted other lineages only when present in sufficient density to impose crowding interference on alternate morphologies --- a phenomenon of biogenic niche construction.
Evolutionary replay experiments abating self-context confirmed that the lineage's complexity was maintained by this biogenic niche construction.
Similar scenarios arose across many replicates, producing a correlation between niche construction and complexity --- while, as before, no direct association arose between complexity and fitness.
These findings confirm that functional complexity can accumulate in multicell lineages due to environment-scale biotic contingencies that impose adaptive selection on otherwise arbitrary traits.
Such an ecological framing of complex multicellularity notably bridges adaptationist and neutralist hypotheses: when ecological niche construction acts as the ratchet for generative entrenchment, traits can be stabilized by genuine adaptive benefit, rather than solely the deleterious side effects of loss.
\end{abstract}
