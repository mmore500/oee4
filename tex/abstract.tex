\begin{abstract}
Although complex multicellularity is foundational to macroscopic life, theory explaining its emergence remains hotly contested and difficult to test experimentally.
In particular, challenges remain in resolving adaptationist and neutralist framings of complex multicellularity.
The former explains complex multicellularity as arising from positive selection for synergistic benefits of division of labor and economies of scale in somatic tissues, while the latter emphasizes drift accumulation of arbitrary functional contingencies under selection weakened by small population sizes.
We examined this issue using a model system of digital organisms: computer programs that evolve as they replicate, mutate, and compete in forming group structures akin to multicellular bodies.
Across evolutionary trials, high-complexity lineages of digital organisms exhibited no inherent fitness advantage --- consistent with neutralist expectations.
However, closer analysis of a lineage producing sophisticated group morphologies revealed a different driver of complexity.
Competition assays showed this lineage performed best only when serving as its own environmental context --- a phenomenon of self-acting niche construction.
Replay experiments abating self-context confirmed that the lineage's extreme complexity was maintained by its emergent biotic selection effects.
As demonstrated here in a concrete instance, such an ecological framing of multicell evolution --- wherein complexity accumulates as new environment-scale biotic contingencies impose adaptive selection on otherwise arbitrary traits --- suggests a path toward reconciliation of adaptationist and neutralist perspectives.
\end{abstract}
