\begin{abstract}
Complex multicellularity exemplifies the surprising constructive power of evolution.
% morphological and physiological
% successive
While natural history evidences extensive innovation in form and function along some multicell lineages, evolutionary processes driving such complexity remain opaque.
As a tractable model for broad aspects of multicell evolution, we investigated this issue using cell-like digital organisms: computer programs that evolve as they replicate, mutate, and compete or cooperate.
% In evolutionary trials where digital organisms could form group structures akin to multicellular bodies, fitness advantage arose from neither quantity of adaptive genome content nor quantity of adaptive phenotypic traits.
% Long-standing curiosity, in particular, persists as to how adaptive selection and neutral ratcheting interact to produce complexity in these lineages.
Among evolutionary outcomes, we observed an extreme outlier in complexity measures which exhibited patterned multicellular growth and multi-stage life history.
% Across replicates, we observed
Case study analysis revealed biogenic niche construction, which imposed collective crowding interference on alternate morphologies.
Neutralizing biogenic context in replay experiments revealed that this case study lineage, in fact, exerted selection to maintain its own complexity.
% Analogous scenarios arose across replicates
Cross-replicate analysis suggests such ecological lock-in of complexity was widespread, with correlation between niche construction and functional complexity matching or exceeding the effects of adaptation and drift.
These findings show how biotic selection can act as a powerful complexity ratchet, and concretely demonstrate a possibly critical role of eco-evolutionary feedback in early evolution of biological multicellularity.
\end{abstract}
