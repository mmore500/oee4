\begin{abstract}
Complex multicellularity exemplifies the surprising constructive power of evolution.
% morphological and physiological
% successive
While genetic and fossil evidence show extensive innovation in form and function along some multicell lineages, the evolutionary processes driving such complexity remain opaque.
As a tractable model system to capture broad aspects of multicell evolution, we investigated this issue using cell-like digital organisms: computer programs that evolve as they replicate, mutate, and compete or cooperate.
% In evolutionary trials where digital organisms could form group structures akin to multicellular bodies, fitness advantage arose from neither quantity of adaptive genome content nor quantity of adaptive phenotypic traits.
% Long-standing curiosity, in particular, persists as to how adaptive selection and neutral ratcheting interact to produce complexity in these lineages.
Among evolutionary outcomes, we observed an extreme outlier in complexity measures that exhibited patterned cell growth and multi-stage life history.
% Across replicates, we observed
Case study analysis of this high-complexity lineage revealed biogenic niche construction, wherein the evolved multicells imposed collective crowding interference on alternate morphologies.
Evolutionary replay experiments neutralizing this biogenic context reveal that the case study strain in fact exerted selection to maintain its own complexity.
% Analogous scenarios arose across replicates
Cross-replicate analysis suggests such ecological lock-in was widespread, producing a correlation between niche construction and functional complexity --- similar in magnitude to, or stronger than, the effects of adaptation and drift.
These findings show how biotic selection can act as a powerful complexity ratchet, and concretely demonstrate a potentially critical role of eco-evolutionary feedback in early evolution of biological multicellularity.
\end{abstract}
