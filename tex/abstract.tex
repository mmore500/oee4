\begin{abstract}
Complex multicellularity exemplifies the surprising constructive power of evolution.
Long-standing curiosity, in particular, persists as to how adaptive selection and neutral ratcheting interact to produce complexity in these lineages.
We investigated this issue using a model system of digital organisms: computer programs that evolve as they replicate, mutate, and compete.
In evolutionary trials where these digital organisms could form group structures akin to multicellular bodies,
no inherent fitness advantage arose from either quantity of adaptive genome content or quantity of adaptive phenotypic traits.
However, closer analysis of a  high-complexity lineage exhibiting patterned growth revealed a different evolutionary driver of complexity.
This lineage outcompeted other lineages only when present in sufficient density to impose crowding interference on alternate morphologies --- a phenomenon of biogenic niche construction.
Evolutionary replay experiments neutralizing self-context confirmed that the lineage's complexity was maintained by biogenic niche construction.
Similar scenarions arose across many replicates, producing a correlation between niche construction and complexity --- while, as before, no direct association arose between complexity and fitness.
These findings confirm that functional complexity can accumulate in multicell lineages due to environment-scale biotic contingencies that impose adaptive selection on otherwise arbitrary traits.
Such an ecological framing of complex multicellularity notably bridges adaptationist and neutralist hypotheses: when ecological niche construction acts as the ratchet for generative entrenchment, traits can be stabilized by genuine adaptive benefit, rather than solely the deleterious consequences of contingent lock-in.
\end{abstract}
