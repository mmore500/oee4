Complexity and novelty are not inevitable outcomes of Darwinian evolution \citep{stanley2017open}.
Instead, how and why some lineages evolve novel traits and complex functionality merits explanation.

For this purpose, artificial model systems provide special utility in testing, through concrete experiments, whether sets of mechanistic building blocks are sufficient to reproduce outcomes observed in nature.
Here, using a digital organism model of multicell evolution, we discovered high levels of genetic and phenotypic complexity stabilized by spontaneous niche construction effects --- under a basic set of mechanistic assumptions, largely shared in common with natural life.

Along a case study lineage with high sustained complexity, we replayed evolution with abated biotic context to directly test the causal role of niche construction in maintaining evolved complexity --- and traced a detailed account of how complexity, novelty, and adaptation developed across historical intermediates.

Out of necessity, modern biological science relies extensively on work with model systems.
Indisputably, digital organisms differ from biological life in fundamental aspects of genetic makeup, biophysical constraints, and environmental conditions --- and, unlike natural models, digital organisms lack the core biochemical homologies of the sort that link studies of \textit{E. coli} and the elephant.
However, because digital evolution models and natural life nonetheless share core processes of reproduction, mutation, inheritance, and competition, they thus both undergo the same fundamental process of evolution by natural selection.
On account of dynamic genome expression mechanisms, both systems can also exhibit rugged, nonlinear fitness landscapes, plasticity, and flexible information processing behavior \citep{TODO}.
Thus, like any model system, digital findings demonstrate what is possible and open the door to further tests of generality \citep{TODOflattest}.

To remain computationally tractable, our experiments do not reach vast generational timescales and large population sizes characteristic of biological evolution.
Contemporary high-performance computing hardware --- leveraged here only at the single-node level --- offers immense potential to scale up digital evolution experiments, and harnessing them to explore how evolved complexity scales across time and space is an exciting direction for future work \citep{TODO}.
Though we saw substantial growth in genome size and redundancy over the surveyed period of our case study, neutral evolution, in particular, should exert a cumulative influence that intensifies over longer timescales.

Despite these limitations, our reported experiments open a new window into possible ecological dynamics shaping early evolution of complex multicellularity, a threshold simultaneously unlocking vast new morphological possibilities and niche opportunities \citep{TODO}.
While co-evolutionary arms race dynamics are well-recognized drivers of body enlargement and other complex traits \citep{TODO}, our experiments reveal an alternate ecological pathway to complexity: adaptive entrenchment of new traits through niche construction effects.
Specifically, we found that multicell adaptation to biotic environmental context can stabilize complexity underlying those evolved traits.
This result extends the logic of constructive neutral evolution to the ecosystem level \citep{muozgmez2021constructive}, with the ratchet mechanisms of generative entrenchment acting via external niche construction rather than internal epistatic dependencies.
Interestingly, such a mechanism captures aspects of both adaptationist and neutralist explanations for the evolution of complexity --- combining the active driving force of selection with passive structural lock-in of historical contingency.
In concert with broader progress in experimental evolution, capturing emergent ecological dynamics of early multicellularity \textit{de novo} helps bridge dilemmas in theory beyond the hypothetical plausibility of abstract scenarios, challenging observers to explain outcomes of evolution in action in concrete instances.
