\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Novelty and complexity are not inevitable outcomes of Darwinian evolution \citep{stanley2017open}.
Instead, how and why some multicellular lineages evolve novel traits and complex functionality merits explanation.
% within some systems, under some conditions multicellular
% For this purpose, artificial model systems provide special utility in testing whether particular sets of mechanistic building blocks are sufficient to reproduce outcomes observed in nature.
Here, using a digital organism model, we found that complex traits underlying patterned growth can --- as a side effect --- promote their own maintenance by reshaping environmental context.
That is, complex multicellularity can be stabilized by niche construction effects.
Examining a high-complexity lineage with patterned morphology, we found that biogenic environmental structure arising from group formations selected to maintain them by disadvantaging other lineages.
This pattern arose broadly across replicates, with those exhibiting specific adaptation to self-context exhibiting greater functional complexity.

As a model, digital organisms can reflect dynamic plasticity, rugged fitness landscapes, flexible information processing behavior, and Darwinian evolution characteristic of natural life \citep{pennock2007models}.
Nonetheless, given profound differences between the two, digital findings are only a first step to further tests of generality \citep{Wilke2001,Codoer2006} --- like any model system.
For instance, while the particular crowding mechanism seen in our case study is likely substrate-specific, biological multicells exhibit many richer mechanistic opportunities for biotic selection and niche construction.

To remain computationally tractable, our experiments also forgo the vast timescales and population sizes characteristic of biological evolution.
Neutral processes, in particular, should exert a cumulative influence on complexity that intensifies over these longer timescales.
In future work, high-performance computing hardware --- leveraged here only at single-machine scale --- may soon make such explorations feasible \citep{ackley2014indefinitely,moreno2024trackable,heinemann2007artificial}.

Despite these limitations, reported experiments open a new window into possible ecological dynamics of early multicellularity --- a transition that simultaneously unlocks vast morphological possibilities and vast niche opportunities \citep{Carroll2001}.
While co-evolutionary arms race dynamics are well-recognized drivers of body enlargement and other complex traits \citep{Tong2022,Dawkins1979}, our experiments reveal an alternate ecological pathway to complexity: adaptive entrenchment through niche construction.
% Specifically, we found that complex traits in multicell lineages can be stabilized by biotic selection emerging from those very same traits.

This result suggests that the logic of constructive neutral evolution may extend to the ecosystem level \citep{muozgmez2021constructive}.
Rather than lock-in through within-organism epistatic dependencies, this regime's ratchet mechanism of generative entrenchment would instead act via biotic dependencies among organisms \citep{Waddington2008}.
Notably, such a mechanism combines the active driving force of selection with passive lock-in of arbitrary contingencies, thus blending adaptationist and neutralist hypotheses.
Such effects need not only be self-acting --- as appeared to occur most readily in our model system --- and it is interesting to speculate how such effects may generalize to networks of interacting lineages.
Even in cases where niche construction is only transiently stable, scaffolded biotic traits may nonetheless persist by entraining lasting lower-level, within-organism epistatic entrenchment.
