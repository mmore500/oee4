\section{Results}

\subsection{Case-study population}

Figures \labelcref{fig:16005-timelapse:genotype-complexity,fig:16005-timelapse:phenotype-complexity} compare trajectories of genetic and phenotypic complexity across our 40 evolutionary replicates, measured at 10-stint intervals using genetic knockout and phenotype interference assays.
Among these trials, one replicate (blue highlight) stood out in exhibiting high levels of sustained complexity --- with mean values of both phenotype and genotype complexity falling outside 3$\times$ IQR (Figure \ref{fig:16005-timelapse:mean-complexity}).
The lineage in question also exhibited a distinct multi-stage multicellular life history, shown as a timelapse in lower panels of Figure \ref{fig:16005-timelapse} \labelcref{fig:16005-timelapse:update64,fig:16005-timelapse:update256,fig:16005-timelapse:update832,fig:16005-timelapse:update6720,fig:16005-timelapse:update7616}).
Growing from unicellular inoculum (\labelcref{fig:16005-timelapse:update64}), groups first elongate horizontally to establish an asymmetrical cigar-like morphology (Figure \labelcref{fig:16005-timelapse:update256,fig:16005-timelapse:update832}).
Then, after a delayed secondary growth phase, groups expand in the vertical direction to establish an irregular ``box'' morphology (Figure \labelcref{fig:16005-timelapse:update6720,fig:16005-timelapse:update7616}).

\subsubsection{Morphology}

% Supplementary Table \ref{tab:morph_descriptions} provides more detailed descriptions of each qualitative morph category, as well as a video and still image example of each.
% Supplementary Table \ref{tab:morph_by_stint} provides morph categorization for each stint as well as links to view the stint's specimen in a video or in-browser web simulation.
To investigate how complexity arose and stabilized in this lineage, we inspected its history in detail as a case study.
As representative specimens, we sampled a genome from each passaged population snapshot.
Growing each genome in monoculture, we categorized them into nine qualitative morphologies (``$b$'' through ``$j$'')
\unskip\footnote{%
Descriptions of morphs $h$ and $f$, which were sampled rarely, appear in supplementary material.
}
--- summarized as the top strip of Figure \ref{fig:keyfig} and cataloged in detail in Supplementary Tables \labelcref{tab:morph_descriptions,tab:morph_by_stint} and Supplementary Figure \ref{fig:phylo_parsimony_tree}).
Sampled genomes from early stints mostly grew as an amalgam of unicells and small groups up to 4 cells (morph $b$), although our earliest sample from the case study lineage formed oversized groups through unchecked cell proliferation (morph $c$, stint 2).
At stint 15, morph $e$ arose independently from morph $b$ --- exhibiting horizontal cigar-like patterning.
Earlier, at stint 14, we also saw large group formation (morph $d$, irregular clumps of 10 to 15 cells), but the sample's lineage appears to have subsequently gone extinct.
At stint 45, groups began exhibiting a phase of vertical growth (morph $g$).
Around stint 60, morph $e$ was sampled again as a reversion from morph $g$.
However, morph $g$ continued to be observed as late as stint 90, having reestablished from morph $e$ intermediates.
Finally, at stint 100, we observed a first instance of morph $j$.
This sample resembled the two-phase growth pattern of morph $g$, except that some groups produced horizontal-bar propagules during the secondary ``box'' phase of morphology.

\subsubsection{Complexity}

Next, we explored how these morphological novelties coincided with changes in complexity in the case study lineage.
To explore this relationship, we assayed genotype and phenotype complexity of sampled specimens across the 100-stint lineage history (Figure \ref{fig:keyfig}, Supplementary Figure \ref{fig:complexity}).
Interestingly, these measures identify several episodes where complexity increases as an abrupt spike, in addition to more gradual changes.
For instance, the origination of morphology $d$ coincided with a spike in genetic complexity (25 to 43 sites), while the origination of morphology $e$ coincided with a phenotype complexity spike (5 to 17 interactions).
Substantial new complexity did not always accompany novel morphology, though, as was the case for morphologies $g$ and $j$.
Reciprocally, as well, an abrupt increase in genetic complexity occurred at stint 12 (11 to 27 sites) without accompanying qualitative morphological novelty.

Shifting focus from discrete events to longer-term trends, split regression analysis suggests genetic complexity developed in two distinct regimes ($p < 10^{-14}$): increasing until stint 34 ($p < \text{TODO}$), and then decreasing ($p < 10^{-5}$) (Supplementary Figure \ref{fig:complexity-cryptic-regression:split}).
With respect to the latter regime of decreasing genetic complexity, one possible explanation --- of course --- is actual atrophy of underlying genome functionality.
However, it is also possible that genome complexity simply became harder to detect due to development of mutational robustness that surmounted the limited sensitivity of single-site knockouts.
To test this hypothesis, we ``skeletonized'' genomes by removing neutral sites then repeated our single-site knockout complexity assay.
Genome complexity remained similar after skeletonization, but the trend of decreasing complexity was weakened ($p = 0.06$) --- especially beyond stint 40 ($p = 0.56$) (Supplementary Figures \labelcref{fig:complexity-cryptic-regression:gt34,fig:complexity-cryptic-regression:gt40}).
Thus, after accounting for changes in robustness, the long-term trajectory of genetic complexity in the case study lineage appears to be initial growth followed by stabilization.

\subsubsection{Adaptation}

Next, we sought to explore the relationship between morphological novelties and adaptation in the high-complexity case study lineage.
For this purpose, we competed representatives from successive stints to test their relative fitness.
Across 100 of these competition assays, 57 sampled genotypes outcompeted their predecessor by a significant margin (Figure \ref{fig:keyfig}, Supplementary Figures \labelcref{fig:median_fitness_differential_symlog,TODO}).
In particular, originations of morphs ($d$, $e$, and $g$) were accompanied by fitness gains.
Surprisingly, though, the magnitude of fitness benefit from these morphological innovations appears largely indistinguishable from other stint-to-stint adaptation \citep{hochberg2017innovation} --- even though we did detect large discrete fitness gains at other time points, such as a genome size expansion at stint 93.
% Fitness differentials during the first 40 stints are generally higher magnitude than later fitness differentials, although a strong fitness differential occurs at stint 93 coincident with a genome duplication.

Besides the 57 adaptive changes discussed above, 23 were neutral, and --- surprisingly --- 20 indicated significant fitness loss (Figure \ref{fig:keyfig}).
To assess whether these apparent fitness losses were sampling artifacts, we repeated assays to compete whole-population samples (rather than monoclonal inocculates of individual sampled genotypes).
However, whole-population competition results remained similar: 50 assays indicated significant fitness gain, 34 were neutral, and 16 indicated significant fitness loss (Figure \ref{fig:keyfig}).

Although substantially rarer than fitness gains, these assays would nonetheless suggest nearly one in five collected lineage intermediates were maladaptive.
One possible contributor to maladaptation is drift effects \citep{TODO}, which strengthen in small populations.
Given carrying capacity was only around 500 multicell groups for high-complexity isolates from the case study lineage, such drift effects should indeed be expected.
However, apparent maladaptation may arise as an artifact of other adaptations responsive to exogenous environmental change \citep{brady2019causes}.
We investigate this possibility next.

\subsubsection{Ecological Adaptation}

\input{fig/sign-change-prefatory-population}

Recall that experimental configuration maintained long-term co-existence of an additional independent lineage alongside the case study strain (Figure \ref{fig:stints}).
Therefore, we next sought to test whether co-evolutionary interactions with this ecological background account for apparent maladaptations observed in stint-to-stint competitions.
For this purpose, we repeated our earlier stint $n$ vs. stint $n + 1$ competition assays to incorporate the independent co-evolved lineage as a ``biotic background'' --- comprising half the initial well-mixed population (Figure \ref{fig:fit:bbg}).
\unskip\footnote{%
For this purpose, we used genomes collected from the co-evolved lineage at ``prefatory'' stint $n$, rather than $n + 1$.
Results using co-evolved genomes from ``contemporary'' stint $n + 1$ were generally similar, and are included in supplementary material.
}

After incorporating the background strain into our measure of fitness, we detected only 3 whole-population deleterious outcomes --- instead of 16 (Figure \ref{fig:sign-change-prefatory-population}).
Lower assay sensitivity might partially explain this result, as no fitness effect was detected in nearly twice as many cases (23 vs. 53).
However, 6 cases do provide strong, direct evidence of a sign-change fitness effect from the co-evolved strain.
At these time points, apparently deleterious evolutionary steps actually turned out beneficial in the presence of co-evolved ecological context.
We saw no instances of the reverse, as all 3 outcomes deleterious in ecological context were also deleterious without it.

\subsection{Fitness Effects of Biotic Context in Case Study Strain}

\input{fig/fitness-background}

% Having discovered sign-change fitness interactions between the high-complexity case study strain and its co-evolved ecological context,
As a putative explanation for development and maintenance of complexity in the case study strain, we next sought to identify how the case study strain's co-evolved ecological context exerted biotic selection on it.
As a first step, we set out to test whether these two strains interacted cooperatively or antagonistically.

For this purpose, we needed an absolute (rather than relative) measure of fitness.
As a proxy for absolute fitness, we created an arbitrary ``measuring stick'' for each stint by pooling genome samples from each of our 40 independent replicates.
To measure a genotype's fitness, then, we competed that genotype against all members of the pool --- producing a mean result between 0 (all losses) and 1 (all wins).

The solid line in Figure \ref{fig:fitness-background} shows baseline, no-ecological-context fitness of the case study strain: fitness is volatile during early evolution, but settles around or below 0.5 from stint 30 onwards --- meaning that the case study strain tends to be outcompeted by samples from other replicates.

To measure the effect of co-evolved ecological context on fitness of the case-study lineage, we repeated our measuring stick competitions in a half-and-half mix with the case study strain's co-evolved lineage.
As shown in Figure \ref{fig:fitness-background}, fitness in the context of co-evolved ecology for the most part closely tracks baseline fitness --- although in a number of brief episodes, strong fitness advantages are gained.
In one episode, at stint TODO, co-evolved ecological context imposes a strong fitness penalty.
These results --- in conjunction with further experiments testing how the case study strain, in turn, influences fitness of its co-evolved counterpart (Supplementary Figure \ref{fig:TODO}) --- suggest a relationship of transient commensalist interactions between the two strains, rather than systematic cooperation or antagonism.

Lastly, we assayed fitness using self-context comparator competitions, shown as a second dotted line in Figure \ref{fig:fitness-background}.
In these competitions, initial concentration of the case study strain was effectively boosted from 50\% to 75\%.
To our surprise, although originally intended as a control treatment, self-context produced strong, sustained fitness benefit from stint 15 onward (TODO test, $p < 10^{25}$; \ref{fig:fitness-background:TODO}).
The strength of this effect --- and its coincidence with initial establishment of the cigar-like morphology $e$ --- led us to shift focus toward an alternate putative explanation for the case study strain's high sustained complexity: self-acting biotic selection stemming from the case study strain itself.

\subsection{Self-acting Selection in Case Study Strain}

\input{fig/timeshift}

Having identified that the case study strain developed an advantage in self-dense contexts, we next sought to assess whether this frequency-dependent fitness benefit produced self-acting selection.
If such an effect arose, it could be a candidate for explaining the case study strain's high sustained complexity.

As a test for self-selection, we set out to assess whether the case study strain adapted to enhance the strength of its self-benefit.
For this purpose, we repeated fitness assays against our ``measuring stick'' of cross-replicate comparators.
In these new assays, we time-shifted self-context background.
That is, for each stint $n$ we performed competitions in the presence of case study strain samples gathered from fixed timepoints at stint 33, 40, 50, 66, 75, 90, and 99 --- rather than simply using more of the case study strain at stint $n$ (i.e., exact contemporary background).

Comparing outcomes between contemporary and time-shifted backgrounds, exact contemporary self-context did indeed provide substantially greater fitness benefit (Mann-Whitney $U$ Test, $p<10^{-5}$, $\delta=0.45$; Figure \ref{fig:timeshift}).
Nonetheless, time-shifted backgrounds did still provide some benefit in 78 of 98 tested cases.
% --- including long temporal offsets of up to 50 stints (Supplementary Figure \ref{fig:timeshift-supp}).
Thus, while the underlying mechanism of self-benefit appears to have remained somewhat stable, self-selection --- surprisingly --- did not freeze the phenotypic characteristics that create self-benefit opportunity.
Rather, these characteristics shifted appreciably over time, and imposed moving-optimum selection for related traits necessary to take advantage of self-benefit opportunity.

\subsection{Self-acting Selection Maintains Complexity in Case Study Strain}

\input{fig/replay-background}

After confirming the existence of adaptation to self-context, we turned to test whether selection imposed by the case study strain --- as its own self-context --- contributed to maintenance of its complexity.
To maximize causal rigor, we used evolutionary replay trials to carry out an intervention-based experiment,
% rather than a retrospective analysis.
% Our goal in this experiment was to screen for differences in end-state complexity between treatments including versus excluding self-context
That is, we set out to directly compare complexity outcomes in the presence of case study strain self-context against those in its absence.

Using this experimental design, however, obliged a key practical challenge: some mechanism was needed to remove, or at least meaningfully abate, self-context.
This problem is nontrivial because including the case study strain is necessary to test response in complexity outcomes, but inherently introduces some level of self-context.
To overcome this challenge, we applied mutation to the focal strain population when seeding replay trials, in order to attenuate self-context.
The mutational dose of 8 mutations was chosen such that most (but not all) of seeded genomes were substantially disrupted.
As a useful side-effect, this treatment also magnified standing variation of intact complexity in the test populations --- improving our assay's sensitivity in detecting selection to maintain complexity.
% providing sufficient sensitivity to allow short 1-stint replays.

As schematized in Figure \ref{fig:replay-background:design}, replays were conducted both under a ``self context'' treatment (seeded 50\% with intact case study strain genotypes) and an ``ecological context'' treatment (seeded 50\% with genotypes from a co-evolved lineage).
To target the complexity-maintenance period of case study identified by split regression analysis, replays were conducted from case study strain populations at stint 33 onwards --- thus providing a sample size of 67 1-stint replays per treatment.
At the end of each replay, we sampled a genotype from descendants of the mutagenized case study inoculum and measured its complexity.

Across replays, self context indeed preserved greater genetic complexity in the case study strain, compared to co-evolved ecological context
(Mann-Whitney $U$ test, $p < 0.01$, $\delta = 0.25$; Figure \ref{fig:replay-background:moderatedose}).
Importantly, this result provides direct evidence that biotic selection effects exerted by the case study strain acted to stabilize its own complexity.

As a control treatment, we repeated replay experiments with a higher dose of 50 mutations --- sufficient to ensure substantial disruption of all seeded case study genomes.
% applied to seeded case study strain genotypes.
Under these conditions, complexity outcomes were indistinguishable between self and ecological context treatments (Figure \ref{fig:replay-background:highdose}).
Thus, the case study strain’s biotic effects appear specific to maintaining its particular evolved complexity.
In other words, a niche construction effect --- with the case study strain effectively acting to ``lock in'' complexity by advantaging its own evolved functionality, rather than simply cultivating generic conditions conducive to complexity (e.g., weakening selection).

\subsection{Self Adaptation Drives Functional Complexity}

\input{fig/fit-advantage-correlations}

Given the role of niche construction in maintaining the high evolved complexity of the case study strain, we next sought to assess whether this pattern of self adaptation driving complexity manifested more broadly across all replicates.

To screen for this effect, we measured self adaptation and complexity at 10-stint intervals across all 40 replicates.
Self adaptation was calculated as the difference between comparative fitness measurements in self and co-evolved contexts.

Comparing across replicates, mean self adaptation correlates positively with mean genotype and genotype complexity (Wald tests; $p = 0.01$ and $p = 5\times10^{-3}$; panels \labelcref{fig:fit-advantage-correlations:genotype,fig:fit-advantage-correlations:phenotype}).
For genotype complexity, however, we found this effect strongly focused in early evolution (stints 10-50; Wald test, $p=8\times10^{-6}$; $R^2=0.4$), but dissipate during later time periods (stints 60-100; Wald test, $p=0.3$).

By contrast, phenotype complexity --- which reflects the richness of evolved input/output operation functionality --- correlated significantly with self adaptation across both time periods, and in fact strengthened somewhat during the late time period compared to the early time period (Wald tests; $p=0.05$, $=0.01$; $R^2=0.1,$ $=0.16$).

Despite relationships of complexity to self adaptation, however, neither genotype complexity nor phenotype complexity correlated with fitness (panels \labelcref{fig:fit-advantage-correlations:genotype,fig:fit-advantage-correlations:phenotype}).
In fact, potentially reflecting the load of deleterious mutations, more complex genotypes appeared marginally less fit.

Thus, complexity does appear to be adaptive, but driven specifically by constructed niche context.
Genome complexity associates significantly with self adaptation only during early evolution; it is possible that this relationship is eroded by selection for robustness in the strains with more critical genome sites (as was seen in the case study strain) or by the accumulation of neutral contingent complexity in the strains with fewer critical genome sites.
Adaptation to constructed niches, however, maintains correlation to phenotype complexity --- indicating that this mechanism continues to preserve functional complexity, in particular.
