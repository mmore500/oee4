\section{Results}

\subsection{Case-study population}

Figures \labelcref{fig:16005-timelapse:genotype-complexity,fig:16005-timelapse:phenotype-complexity} compare genotype and phenotype complexity across our 40 evolutionary replicates, measured using genetic knockouts and phenotype interference assays.  % at 10-stint intervals
Among these trials, extreme outlying complexity arose in one replicat withh mean sustained complexity exceeding 3$\times$ IQR for both measures (blue highlight, Figure \ref{fig:16005-timelapse:mean-complexity}).
The lineage in question also exhibited patterned growth in a multi-stage life history.
Growing from unicellular inoculum (Figure \labelcref{fig:16005-timelapse:update64}), groups first elongate horizontally to establish an asymmetrical cigar-like morphology (Figure \labelcref{fig:16005-timelapse:update256,fig:16005-timelapse:update832}).
Then, in a secondary phase of growth, groups expand in the vertical direction to establish an irregular ``box-like'' morphology (Figure \labelcref{fig:16005-timelapse:update6720,fig:16005-timelapse:update7616}).

To investigate how complexity arose and stabilized in this lineage, we investigated its history in detail as a case study --- reported next.

\subsubsection{Complexity}

Next, we assayed genotype and phenotype complexity across lineage history (Figure \labelcref{fig:keyfig:gcomplexity,fig:keyfig:pcomplexity}, Supplementary Figure \ref{fig:complexity}).

In several instances, complexity increased abruptly with new morphology.
For instance, at stint 14, morph $d$ (irregular clumps of 10 to 15 cells) coincided with a spike in genotype complexity from 28 to 44 sites.
At stint 15, morph $e$ grew phenotype complexity from 5 to 17 interactions.

% In two independent sublineages --- both going extinct --- genotype and phenotype complexity simultaneously and abruptly crashed near zero.
% Both were morph $i$: unrestricted cell proliferation with small irregular groups.
% No complexity crash, however, appears in assays configured with biotic background --- suggesting a contingent dependency to this background may have developed (Supplementary Figure \ref{fig:TODO}).

In contrast to these abrupt innovations, genotype complexity also grew in a more gradual phase from 27 to 55 sites between stints 16 and 39.

% https://github.com/mmore500/oee4/blob/binder-2025-11-30-genome-complexity-regression.ipynb/binder/2025-11-30-genome-complexity-regression.ipynb
Across the case study lineage's history, split regression analysis suggests genotype complexity developed in two distinct regimes (Davies test, $p < 10^{-14}$, adj. $R^2=0.43$): increasing until stint 34 (Wald test, $R^2 = 0.59$, $p < 10^{-6}$), and then decreasing (Wald test, $R^2 = 0.24$, $p < 10^{-4}$) (Supplementary Figure \ref{fig:complexity-cryptic-regression:split}).
It is possible, though, that genotype complexity simply became harder to detect in later stints due to development of mutational robustness.
To test this possibility, we ``skeletonized'' genomes by removing unexpressed sites then repeated our single-site knockout complexity assay.
This ``deep'' genotype complexity measure remained generally similar, but the trend of decreasing complexity was weakened ($p = 0.06$) --- especially from stint 40 onwards ($p = 0.56$) (Supplementary Figures \labelcref{fig:complexity-cryptic-regression:geq34,fig:complexity-cryptic-regression:geq40}).
Thus, the evolutionary trajectory of genetic complexity appears to be initial growth followed by stabilization.

\subsubsection{Adaptation}

Next, we examined fitness changes along the case study lineage.
For this purpose, we competed successive representatives to test for fitness gains.
Across 100 such assays, 57 sampled genotypes outcompeted their predecessor by a significant margin (Figure \ref{fig:keyfig:morph}, Supplementary Figures \labelcref{fig:median_fitness_differential_symlog,TODO}).
Fitness gains became somewhat more sparse at later time points --- a notable exception, though, being a strong fitness gain from 500-site genome expansion at stint 93.
% Fitness differentials during the first 40 stints are generally higher magnitude than later fitness differentials, although a strong fitness differential occurs at stint 93 coincident with a genome duplication.

Besides the 57 adaptive steps discussed above, 23 steps were neutral and --- surprisingly --- 20 were deleterious (Figure \ref{fig:keyfig:morph}).
To assess whether these apparent fitness losses were artifacts of within-population variation, we repeated assays to compete whole-population samples rather than monoclonal isolates.
However, whole-population competition results remained similar: 50 assays indicated significant fitness gain, 34 were neutral, and 16 indicated significant fitness loss (Supplementary Figure \ref{fig:keyfig-Prefatory}).

Although significantly rarer than fitness gains ($p < 10^{-4}$; exact Binomial test), nearly one in five lineage intermediates this appeared maladaptive.
While such maladaptation could occur through drift effects (carrying capacity supported only around 500 multicell groups in the case study lineage), such apparent maladaptation may also arise from trade-offs imposed by exogenous environmental factors \citep{brady2019causes}.
We investigate this possibility next.

\subsection{Adaptation to Co-evolved Ecological Context}

\input{fig/sign-change-prefatory-population}

Recall that our experimental configuration maintained long-term co-existence of an additional independent lineage alongside the case study strain.
To test whether co-evolutionary interactions with this background strain could account for apparent maladaptations, we repeated earlier competitions to incorporate the co-evolved lineage as a biotic background (Figure \ref{fig:fit:bbg}).
% \unskip\footnote{%
% For this purpose, we used genomes collected from the co-evolved lineage at ``prefatory'' stint $n$, rather than $n + 1$.
% Results using co-evolved genomes from ``contemporary'' stint $n + 1$ were generally similar, and are included in supplementary material.
% }

After incorporating the co-evolved into our measure of fitness, whole-population maladaptations decreased from 16 to 3 (Figure \ref{fig:sign-change-prefatory-population}).
Lower assay sensitivity might partially explain this result, as no fitness effect was detected in nearly twice as many cases (23 vs. 53).
However, in 6 cases presence of the co-evolved strain changed fitness losses to fitness gains.
% We saw no instances of the reverse, as all 3 outcomes deleterious in ecological context were also deleterious without it.
Thus, co-evolved biotic context exerted at least transient selection on the case study strain.
We next sought to better understand these ecological interactions, as a possible contributor to complexity in the case study strain.

\subsection{Fitness Effects of Biotic Context in Case Study Strain}

\input{fig/fitness-background}

% Having discovered sign-change fitness interactions between the high-complexity case study strain and its co-evolved ecological context,
% As a putative explanation for development and maintenance of complexity in the case study strain, we next sought to identify how the case study strain's co-evolved ecological context exerted biotic selection on it.
To characterize the case study strain's ecological interactions, we first set out to test if they were mutualistic or antagonistic in nature.

%For this purpose, we needed an absolute (rather than relative) measure of fitness.
As a proxy for absolute fitness, we assembled an arbitrary ``measuring stick'' at each stint by drawing genome samples from each of our 40 independent replicates.
To estimate fitness, then, we competed each sample against all members of the pool --- producing a mean result between 0 (all losses) and 1 (all wins).

Excluding biotic context, case study strain fitness is volatile during early evolution, but settles near or below 0.5 from stint 30 onwards (solid line, Figure \ref{fig:fitness-background}) --- meaning that it underperformed most other replicates.

To measure the effect of co-evolved ecological context on fitness, we repeated these measuring stick competitions using the co-evolved lineage as biotic context (dashed line, Figure \ref{fig:fitness-background}).
Fitness in co-evolved context for the most part closely tracked baseline fitness --- though, in several brief episodes, strong fitness advantages appear.
Additionally, in one episode at stint 30, co-evolved ecological context imposed a strong fitness penalty.

These results --- in conjunction with reciprocal experiments testing how the case study strain, in turn, influences fitness of its co-evolved counterpart (Supplementary Figure \ref{fig:TODO}) --- suggest a relationship of transient commensalist interactions between the two strains, rather than systematic cooperation or antagonism.

For completeness, we also assayed fitness using the case study lineage itself as its own environmental context (dot-dash line, Figure \ref{fig:fitness-background}).
These conditions boosted concentration of the case study strain from 50\% to 75\%.
Surprisingly, this self-context produced a strong, sustained fitness benefit from stint 15 onward (Mann-Whitney test, $p < 10^{-25}$; \ref{fig:fitness-background:TODO}).
The strength of this effect --- and its coincidence with cigar-like morphology $e$ --- led us to consider an alternate explanation for the case study strain's complexity: self-acting biotic selection driven by the case study strain itself.

\subsection{Self-acting Selection in Case Study Strain}

\input{fig/timeshift}

Having identified that the case study strain developed an advantage in self-dense contexts, we next sought to assess whether this apparent biogenic niche construction produced self-acting selection.
Such selection, were it to have occured, could then be tested for connections to the case study strain's complexity.

To detect self-acting selection, we tested whether the case study strain's self-benefit exhibited adaptive tracking over time.
For this purpose, we repeated fitness assays against the ``measuring stick'' of cross-replicate comparators.
However, for these new assays, we time-shifted self-context background.
That is, for each stint we performed competitions in the presence of case study strain samples from fixed timepoints at stint 33, 40, 50, 66, 75, 90, and 99 --- rather than contemporary self-context, as before.

Compared to time-shifted self backgrounds, contemporary self-context did indeed provide substantially greater fitness benefit (Mann-Whitney test, $p<10^{-5}$, $\delta=0.45$; Figure \ref{fig:timeshift}).
Nonetheless, time-shifted backgrounds still provided some benefit in 78 of 98 tested cases.
% --- including long temporal offsets of up to 50 stints (Supplementary Figure \ref{fig:timeshift-supp}).
Thus, while the underlying mechanism of self-benefit appears to have remained generally stable, traits responsible for realizing self-benefit shifted over time to adaptively track changes in self-context.

\subsection{Self-acting Selection Maintains Complexity in Case Study Strain}

\input{fig/replay-background}

Seeing evidence for feedback on selection from the case study lineage's biogenic structure, we next turned to test whether this selection favored complexity.
To address this question, we carried out evolutionary replay trials to compare complexity outcomes with and without self-context.

Such an experiment, however, imposes a key practical challenge: some means is needed to remove --- or at least meaningfully abate --- self-context.
This problem is nontrivial, because including the case study strain to test complexity outcomes inherently introduces some level of self-context.
To overcome this challenge, we attenuated the case study strain by applying mutation when seeding replay trials.
We tuned mutational dose size (8 mutations) such that most, but not all, seeded genomes were substantially disrupted.
As an additional benefit, this treatment also enhanced seeded populations' standing variation --- improving our assay's sensitivity to detect selection on complexity.
% providing sufficient sensitivity to allow short 1-stint replays.

Replays were conducted both under a ``self context'' treatment (seeded 50\% with intact case study genotypes) and an ``co-evolved context'' treatment (seeded 50\% with co-evolved genotypes).
To target the complexity maintenance period identified by split regression analysis, replays were conducted from stint 33 onwards --- thus, providing a sample of 67 1-stint replays per treatment.
At the end of each replay, we sampled a genotype descended the mutagenized case study inoculum and measured its complexity.

Across replays, self context preserved greater genotype complexity, compared to co-evolved ecological context
(Mann-Whitney test, $p < 0.01$, $\delta = 0.25$; Figure \ref{fig:replay-background:moderatedose}).
This result conrfirms that biotic selection exerted by the case study strain acted to stabilize its own complexity.

As a control treatment, we repeated replay experiments with a higher dose of 50 mutations --- sufficient to ensure substantial disruption to seeded genomes.
% applied to seeded case study strain genotypes.
Under these conditions, complexity outcomes were indistinguishable between self and ecological context treatments (Figure \ref{fig:replay-background:highdose}).
Thus, the case study strain’s biotic selection effects appear specific to maintaining its own evolved complexity.
In other words, evolved traits acted to ``lock in'' complexity by advantaging themselves, as opposed to simply cultivating generic conditions conducive to complexity (e.g., weakening selection).

\subsection{Self Adaptation Drives Functional Complexity}

\input{fig/fit-advantage-correlations}

Having found that niche construction helped maintain evolved complexity in the case study strain, we next sought to assess whether this pattern manifested more broadly across replicates.

To screen for similar niche construction effects, we measured self adaptation across all 40 replicates, calculated as the difference between ``measuring stick'' fitness in self versus co-evolved contexts.

Across replicates, mean self adaptation correlates positively with mean genotype and phenotype complexity (Wald test; $p = 0.01$ and $p = 5\times10^{-3}$; Supplementary Figure \ref{fig:fit-advantage-correlations-supp}).

For genotype complexity, correlation with self-adaptation was strongly focused in early evolution (stints 10 to 50, Figure \ref{fig:fit-advantage-correlations:early}; Wald test, $p=8\times10^{-6}$; $R^2=0.41$), and dissipated during later time periods (stints 60 to 100, Figure \ref{fig:fit-advantage-correlations:late}; Wald test, $p=0.3$).
By contrast, phenotype complexity --- which reflects the richness of evolved cell interactions --- correlated significantly with self adaptation across both time periods, and in fact strengthened somewhat during the later period (Wald tests; $p=0.05$, $p=0.01$; $R^2=0.1$, $R^2=0.16$; Figure \labelcref{fig:fit-advantage-correlations:early,fig:fit-advantage-correlations:late}).

In line with both adaptationist and neutralist hypotheses for the evolution of complexity, deep genotype complexity --- which includes sites masked by robustness --- correlated with fitness and elapsed duration during the later period (panels \labelcref{fig:fit-advantage-correlations:complexity-drivers}).
Drift effects, in particular, manifested robstly over longer the whole-experiment timespan (Wald test, $p leq 1 \times 10^{-6}$; $R^2 = 0.06$; Supplementary Figure \ref{fig:fit-advantage-correlations-supp}).

Thus, complexity appears to be significantly driven by density-dependent self-adaptation --- a signature of biogenic niche construction in this system.
Although fitness-critical genotype complexity only associated significantly with niche construction during early evolution, niche construction more lastingly stabilized phenotype complexity.
It is likely that later selection for robustness erodes fragile genotype complexity --- as seen in earlier split-regression analysis of the case study strain.
