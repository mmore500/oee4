\section{Results}

\subsection{Case-study population}

Figures \labelcref{fig:16005-timelapse:genotype-complexity,fig:16005-timelapse:phenotype-complexity} compare trajectories of genetic and phenotypic complexity across our 40 evolutionary replicates, measured at 10-stint intervals using genetic knockout and phenotype interference assays.
Among these trials, one replicate (blue highlight) stood out in exhibiting high levels of sustained complexity --- with mean values of both phenotype and genotype complexity falling outside 3$\times$ IQR (Figure \ref{fig:16005-timelapse:mean-complexity}).
The lineage in question also exhibited a distinct multi-stage miulticellular life history, shown as a timelapse in lower panels of Figure \ref{fig:16005-timelapse} \labelcref{fig:16005-timelapse:update64,fig:16005-timelapse:update256,fig:16005-timelapse:update832,fig:16005-timelapse:update6720,fig:16005-timelapse:update7616}).
Growing from unicellular inocculum (\labelcref{fig:16005-timelapse:update64}), groups first elongate horizontally to establish an asymmetrical cigar-like morphology (Figure \labelcref{fig:16005-timelapse:update256,fig:16005-timelapse:update832}).
Then, after a delayed secondary growth phase, groups expand in the vertical direction to establish an irregular ``box'' morphology (Figure \labelcref{fig:16005-timelapse:update6720,fig:16005-timelapse:update7616}).

To investigate how complexity arose and stabilized in this lineage, we inspected its history in detail as a case study.
As representative specimens, we sampled a genome from each passaged population snapshot.
Growing each genome in monoculture, we categorized them into nine qualitative morphologies (``$b$'' through ``$j$'')
\unskip\footnote{%
Descriptions of morphs $h$ and $f$, which were sampled rarely, appear in supplementary material.
}
--- summarized as the top strip of Figure \ref{fig:keyfig} and cataloged in detail in Supplementary Tables \labelcref{tab:morph_descriptions,tab:morph_by_stint} and Supplementary Figure \ref{fig:phylo_parsimony_tree}).
Sampled genomes from early stints mostly grew as an amalgam of unicells and small groups up to 4 cells (morph $b$), although our earliest sample from the case study lineage formed oversized groups through unchecked cell proliferation (moph $c$, stint 2).
At stint 15, morph $e$ arose independently from morph $b$ --- exhibiting horizontal cigar-like patterning.
Earlier, at stint 14, we also saw large group formation (morph $d$, irregular clumps of 10 to 15 cells), but the sample's lineage appears to have subsequently extincted.
At stint 45, groups began exhibiting a phase of vertical growth (morph $g$).
Around stint 60, morph $e$ was sampled sampled again as a reversion from morph $g$.
However, morph $g$ continued to be observed as late as stint 90, having reestablished from morph $e$ intermediates.
Finally, at stint 100, we observed a first instance of morph $j$.
This sample resembled the two-phase growth pattern of morph $g$, except that some groups produced horizontal-bar propagules during the secondary ``box'' phase of morphology.

\subsubsection{Complexity}

Next, we explored how these morphological novelties coincided with changes in complexity in the case study lineage.
To explore this relationship, we assayed genotype and phenotype complexity of sampled specimens across the 100-stint lineage history (Figure \ref{fig:keyfig}, Supplementary Figure \ref{fig:complexity}).
Interestingly, these measures identify several episodes where complexity increases as an abrupt spike, in addition to more gradual changes.
For instance, the origination of morphology $d$ coincided with a spike in genetic complexity (25 to 43 sites), while the originaltino of morphology $e$ coincided with a phenotype complexity spike (5 to 17 interactions).
Substantial new complexity did not always accompany novel morphology, though, as was the case for morphologies $g$ and $j$.
Reciporically, as well, an abrupt increase in genetic complexity occured at stint 12 (11 to 27 sites) without any accompanying qualitative morphological novelty.

Shifting focus from discrete events to longer-term trends, split regression analysis suggests genetic complexity developed in two distinct regimes ($p < 10^{-14}$): increasing until stint 34 ($p < \text{TODO}$), and then decreasing ($p < 10^{-5}$) (Supplementary Figure \ref{fig:complexity-cryptic-regression:split}).
With respect to the latter regime of decreasing genetic complexity, one possible explanation --- of course --- is actual atrophy of underlying genome functionality.
However, it is also possible that genome complexity simply became harder to detect due to development of mutational robustness that surmounted the limited sensitivity of single-site knockouts.
To test this hypothesis, we ``skeletonized'' genomes by removing neutral sites then repeated our single-site knockout complexity assay.
Genome complexity remained similar after skeletonization, but the trend of decreasing complexity was weakened ($p = 0.06$) --- especially beyond stint 40 ($p = 0.56$) (Supplementary Figures \labelcref{fig:complexity-cryptic-regression:gt34,fig:complexity-cryptic-regression:gt40}).
Thus, after accounting for changes in robustness, the long-term trajectory of genetic complexity in the case study lineage appears to be initial growth followed by stabilization.

\subsubsection{Adaptation}

Next, we sought to explore the relationship between morphological novelties and adaptation in the high-complexity case study lineage.
For this purpose, we competed representatives from successive stints to test their relative fitness.
Across 100 of these competition assays, 57 sampled genotypes outcompeted their predecessor by a significant margin (Figure \ref{fig:keyfig}, Supplementary Figures \labelcref{fig:median_fitness_differential_symlog,TODO}).
In particular, originations of morphs ($d$, $e$, and $g$) were were accompanied by fitness gains.
Surprisingly, though, the magnitude of fitness benefit from these morphological innovations appears largely indistinguishable from other stint-to-stint adaptation \citep{hochberg2017innovation} --- even though we did detect large discrete fitness gains at other time points, such as a genome size expansion at stint 93.
% Fitness differentials during the first 40 stints are generally higher magnitude than later fitness differentials, although a strong fitness differential occurs at stint 93 coincident with a genome duplication.

Besides the 57 adaptive changes discussed above, 23 were neutral, and --- surprisingly --- 20 indicated significant fitness loss (Figure \ref{fig:keyfig}).
To assess whether these apparent fitness losses were sampling artifacts, we repeated assays to compete between whole-population samples (rather than monoclonal inocculation with single sampled genotypes).
However, whole-population competition results remained similar: 50 assays indicated significant fitness gain, 34 were neutral, and 16 indicated significant fitness loss (Figure \ref{fig:keyfig}).

Although substantially rarer than fitness gains, these assays would nonetheless suggest nearly one in five collected lineage intermediates were maladaptive.
One possible contributor to maladaptation is drift effects \citep{TODO}, which strengthen in small populations.
Given carrying capacity was only around 500 multicell groups for high-complexity isolates from the case study lineage, such drift effects should indeed be expected.
However, apparent maladaptation may arise as an artifact of other adaptations responsive to exogenous environmental change \citep{brady2019causes}.
We investigate this possibility next.

\subsection{Ecological Interactions}

\input{fig/sign-change-prefatory-population}

Next, we investigated whether the presence of the background strain as a ``biotic background'' influenced fitness.
We repeated the two experiments described above (specimen and population competition assays), but inserted the background strain as half of the initial well-mixed population.

To better characterize the mechanism behind fitness effects caused by the background strain, we performed additional specimen adaptation assays under biotic background conditions with diversity maintenance disabled.
This analysis allowed us to test whether action of the diversity maintenance mechanism, rather than direct interactions between the focal and background strains, caused the observed fitness effects.
Supplementary Figure \ref{fig:with_vs_without_diversity_maintenance} compares adaptation assay outcomes with and without diversity maintenance under both the prefatory and contemporary biotic background conditions.
Outcomes were generally similar, and we observed only one sign-change difference: one specimen outcome was beneficial under prefatory biotic background conditions without diversity maintenance, but deleterious with diversity maintenance.
Further, without diversity maintenance, we still observed four outcomes that were advantageous under biotic conditions but deleterious under abiotic conditions (Supplementary Figure \ref{fig:outcome_count_joint_distns:specimen_no_dm}).
So, biotic selective effects cannot be explained as an artifact of the diversity maintenance scheme.%
\footnote{
We also conducted specimen adaptation assays with diversity maintenance disabled under the control focal strain biotic background.
In these experiments, we again found no evidence for impact from the diversity maintenance scheme on results
(Supplementary Figure \ref{fig:baseline_fitness_gain_or_loss}).
}

After incorporating the background strain into our measure of fitness, we detected fewer whole-population deleterious outcomes --- only six under contemporary biotic background conditions and only three under prefatory biotic background conditions (Supplementary Figure \ref{fig:outcome_count_distns}).
To determine whether the presence of the background strain caused the overall reduction in whole-population deleterious outcomes, we performed control competitions under biotic background conditions, but with the focal strain population substituted for the background strain population (Supplementary Figure \ref{fig:baseline_adaptation_control}).
Under these conditions, nine of the stints where whole-population deleterious outcomes had been detected came up neutral;
one, surprisingly, tested significantly adaptive (Supplementary Figure \ref{fig:baseline_fitness_gain_or_loss}).
Dose-dependent fitness effects and/or reduced experimental sensitivity of the biotic background assay appear to play at least a partial role in explaining the reduction of detected whole-population deleterious outcomes.
However, 10 stints still tested significantly deleterious with the control focal strain biotic background in addition to without biotic background.

Four stints do provide strong, direct evidence of a selective effect by the background strain: four whole-population outcomes that were deleterious without their biotic background were actually significantly advantageous in the presence of both the prefatory and contemporary background strain populations (Supplementary Figure \ref{fig:outcome_count_joint_distns:population}).
All four of these stints exhibited whole-population deleterious outcomes under the control focal strain biotic background, indicating that the observed fitness sign change was specifically due to the presence of the background strain (Supplementary Figure \ref{fig:baseline_fitness_gain_or_loss}).

We also found one whole-population outcome that was significantly advantageous without biotic background and in the presence of the prefatory background strain population but significantly deleterious in the presence of the contemporary background strain, possibly suggesting an ``arms race''-like evolutionary innovation on the part of the background strain over that stint (Supplementary Figure \ref{fig:outcome_count_joint_distns:population}).

Nonetheless, we still saw three whole-population outcomes that were significantly deleterious under all three conditions (Supplementary Figure \ref{fig:outcome_count_joint_distns:population}).
These whole-population outcomes were also deleterious under the control focal strain biotic background experiments (Supplementary Figure \ref{fig:baseline_fitness_gain_or_loss}).
Muller's ratchet \citep{andersson1996muller} or maladaptation due to environmental change \citep{brady2019causes} may offer possible explanations, but a definitive answer will require further study.
