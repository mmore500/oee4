\section{Methods} \label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Experimental System}

\input{fig/overview.tex}

DISHTINY is a software platform for conducting evolution experiments using digital organisms \citep{moreno2019toward}.
% Digital evolution experiments, closely related to agent-based modeling approaches, study populations of computer programs that compete to propagate themselves --- analogous to genomes within biological cells \citep{TODO}.
In DISHTINY, replicating computer programs each occupy a virtual cell in a toroidal 2D environment.
Reproduction occurs by asexual budding to a chosen adjacent position, supplanting any preexisting occupant.
To introduce multicellular structure, DISHTINY allows parent cells creating budded offspring to distinguish between group expansion (i.e., agglomeration; Figure \ref{fig:overview}c) and new group establishment (i.e., propagule generation; Figure \ref{fig:overview}d).
\unskip\footnote{%
In earlier work, we found that more case studies of interest arose when two nested levels of group membership were tracked, as opposed to a single, unnested level of group membership \citep{moreno2021exploring}; we continue this approach here.
Within this two-level framework, cells are given the choice to retain offspring within both groups, to expel offspring from both groups, or to expel offspring from the innermost group only.
}
Although mechanistic constraints impose a single-cell bottleneck on group formation \citep{ratcliff2015origins}, no direct extrinsic steering is imposed on cooperation or conflict within groups.
Cells become eligible to reproduce by passively accruing energy, and we reward group formation by boosting energy inflow.
As a practical matter, to ensure group turnover, groups are broken into unicells after a fixed ``max-out'' duration.

In DISHTINY, neighboring cells interact by competing for space, sharing energy, exchanging messages, or sensing each other's state.
Cell-cell interactions occur both within groups and --- key to permit niche construction --- between groups.
Within-group cell-cell interactions allow dynamic development and patterning processes \citep{goldsby2012task,moreno2021exploring}; between-group cell-cell interactions create an environment of free-form interface competition between abutting groups.

At the level of individual cells, behavior results from expression of tagged program modules within cell genomes \citep{lalejini2018evolving}.
These genome modules selectively activate in response to incoming signals \citep{moreno2023matchmaker,holland2012signals} --- generated by updates to the local environment, cell-cell messages, or expression of other modules within the cell (Figure \ref{fig:overview}g).
DISHTINY cells may dynamically regulate modules' signal-matching priority, facilitating plasticity and differentiation \citep{lalejini2021tag}.
Within modules, programs consist of a linear instruction sequence.
Available instructions include floating point arithmetic operations, conditional control flow operations (e.g., looping, branching, etc.), input operations to sense local state, and output operations to trigger actions (e.g., resource sharing, reproduction, etc.).
The full set of 56 available instruction types is described in supplementary material, alongside details for full simulation logic and parameter settings.

\subsection{Evolutionary Replicates}

Ancestor populations for evolution experiments were randomly generated 100-instruction genomes, typically encoding on the order of 5 signal-response modules.
The configured population size --- 14,400 cells --- reliably sufficed that some founding ancestors were viable replicators.
All replications, however, were subject to possible mutation: point mutations, insertions, deletions, and sequence slip mutations (i.e., duplications or deletions).
Mutations could also increase signal-response module count by introducing new activation entrypoints.

After initialization, competition for space drove most founding strains extinct.
To ensure a heterogeneous biotic environment within each experimental replicate, we imposed a simple diversity balancing scheme by progressively hastening stored energy decay for any strain exceeding half of available carrying capacity.
Thus, replicates converged to maintain at least two founding strains.
Among these, we arbitrarily chose one strain for primary study --- the ``focal'' strain --- and another founding strain was sampled as a ``background'' (or ``co-evolved'') strain.

For compatibility with our compute cluster's scheduling system, evolutionary replicates were passaged in three hour increments, which we refer to as ``stints.''
Each transfer carried over all population genomes, but no other simulation state.
This protocol ensured strains remained capable to grow from well-mixed inoculum.
Figure \ref{fig:stints} summarizes our serial transfer and diversity maintenance procedures.

We conducted 100 passages, amounting to just over 12.5 days of total compute time per each of 40 replicates.
Across replicates, between 5.4M and 9.9M simulation time steps elapsed (mean 7.4M; SD 1.1M).
During this time, along focal lineages between 11.7k and 26.9k cell generations elapsed (mean 18.5k; SD 3.4k) and between 1.2k and 3.4k group generations elapsed (mean 2.3k; SD 533).
In the replicate producing our high-complexity case study, 7,565,309 simulation time steps elapsed --- accreting 11,713 cell generations and 1,325 group generations.
To accelerate the experiment, quadrants of simulation space were distributed across four asynchronous hardware threads for parallel evaluation.
For this reason, phylogeny analyses relied on parsimony-based reconstruction rather than exact tracking \citep{moreno2022hereditary}.
Further technical implementation details appear in supplementary material.

\subsection{Measuring Fitness}
\label{sec:measuringfitness}

\input{fig/fit.tex}

Like nature, cell behavior and fate in DISHTINY emerges from stochastic interaction between expressed genetic traits and the local environment.
% fitness effects of a mutation are not specified or determined \textit{a priori}.
Because fitness thus arises as an implicit outcome, rather than an explicit trait, it is neither specified nor readily determined \textit{a priori} \citep{franklin2019mapping} --- fitness must be evaluated \textit{in situ}.
Further complicating matters, we cannot reliably evaluate fitness just by looking at a cell or group in isolation, on account of dense biotic interaction between groups \citep{wadgymar2024defining}.

Therefore, to assess fitness, we rely on inter-strain competition assays.
\unskip\footnote{%
Supplementary material also reports doubling-time fitness assays, which measure expansion into empty space.
However, as our evolutionary trials begin and remain near carrying capacity, we did not find these assays informative of ability to compete for occupied space.
}
These assays began with available space filled by compared strains in an even mix.
Then, after a 10 minute runtime, we evaluated relative abundances of compared strains (Figure \ref{fig:fit:nobbg}).
\unskip\footnote{%
As a point of reference, in the high-complexity lineage this runtime elapsed between 8,000 update cycles (stint 0) and 2,000 update cycles (stint 100).
Further detail in Supplementary Figures \labelcref{fig:num_updates_elapsed_heatmap,fig:num_updates_elapsed_barplot,fig:num_updates_elapsed_boxplot}).
}
To assess significance, we applied a two-tailed binomial exact test to net win count across 20 replicate competitions.

For some analyses, we also used a ``biotic background'' competition design --- similar in principle to mesocosm experiments integrating ecological context \citep{sala2000methods}.
Biotic background assays were seeded by mixing competitors with a ``bystander'' strain in 25\%-25\%-50\% proportion.
Although the bystander strain participates fully in the competition environment, it is then excluded from end-state abundance assessment of competitors (Figure \ref{fig:fit:nobbg}).
In some cases, we purposed this biotic background competition design to test frequency-dependent selection effects; one competitor strain can effectively be updosed by including a facsimile as bystander --- similar in principle to a replacement series experiment \citep{deWit1960competition}.

\subsection{Measuring Complexity}
\label{sec:measuringcomplexity}

Defining and quantifying complexity remains a nuanced, unresolved topic \citep{adami2002what,parrott2010measuring,bottcher2017molecules,tenaillon2007quantifying,bonchev2005complexity,hazen2007functional,marion2015extending,rebout2021measuring}.
Here, we approach complexity as evolved structure that is functionally adaptive.
To measure phenotypic complexity, we counted unique cell input/output interfaces providing context-specific information that boost fitness.
This measure describes the richness of behavior across cell interfaces \citep{mcshea2000functional}, aiming to capture dynamic interaction essential to complex multicellularity \citep{knoll2011multiple,niklas2013origins,brunet2017origin}.
To measure genetic complexity, we count genome sites that contribute to fitness \citep{dolson2019modes,adami2000evolution} as a representation of underlying control structure that direct phenotypic interactions.
\unskip\footnote{%
Promisingly, we find moderate correlation between these measures across replicates and, within the high-complexity case study, also find moderate correlation in evolutionary changes to these measures (Supplementary Figures \labelcref{fig:gpcomplex-correlation,fig:TODO}).
}

Because assessing adaptation through competition experiments is computationally expensive, our complexity measures rely on pragmatic, coarse assay designs.
We estimated phenotype complexity via phenotype manipulation interventions in suite of wildtype-vs-degraded competition trials \citep{sinervo1996testing}, de-contextualizing (i.e., spatially shuffling) each of the 105 available readable ``sensor'' cell inputs and each of the 18 available writable ``actuator'' cell outputs.
Phenotype complexity competition trials also screened for fitness degradation when intercepting and reflecting portions of message-passing traffic.
To estimate genotype complexity, we used single-site knockouts to test the adaptive contribution of individual genome sites.
Because knockout combinations are not tested, this measure is limited in detecting sites with compensatory redundancy or small fitness effects (i.e., below competition sensitivity threshold) \citep{moreno2024methods}.

To tractably measure complexity across many replicates and timepoints, we streamlined workload by evaluating competition outcomes against a shared set of wildtype-vs-wildtype control trials and pre-filtering non-expressed genome sites.
Supplementary materials further detail these procedures, including quality control measures.
