\section{Introduction}

Why should complex multicellular organisms exist, when simpler unicellular organisms grow biomass with substantially greater efficiency \citep{lynch2024bioenergetic}?
Accumulated adaptations for somatic division of labor and economies of scale \unskip\citep{Saunders1976,corning2015synergistic,niklas2014evolutionary,Bell1997} alone cannot fully explain complex multicellularity, as they recoup only a portion of metabolic costs.
Instead, some argue complex multicellular organisms to reflect diffusion or a random walk from a lower complexity bound \citep{Gould1988,gould1990wonderful,gould2011full,mcshea2010biology,Lehman2010,Miconi2008}.
In this framing, modest population sizes among multicells act as a ratchet to accumulate near-neutral redundancies and superficialities that, through gradual accumulation of mechanistic interdependencies, become locked in to essential functionality \citep{lynch2003origins,Smith1972,stoltzfus1999possibility,muozgmez2021constructive}.

An alternate framing of adaptation, however, may complement such explanations: niche construction, where feedback from organisms reshape their own environmental context \citep{odlingsmee2003niche,odlingsmee1988niche,levins1985dialectical} through abiotic ecosystem engineering or direct production of biogenic structure.
Where complex traits gain advantage from new environmental context, they may be promoted by selection \citep{taylor2004niche,Waddington2008,laland2014niche,creanza2016cultural}.
In the case of multicellularity, combinatorially vast morphological possibility opens a Pandora's box of potential biotic interactions.
For instance, it has long been recognized that large body size may provide a means to resist predation \citep{niklas2014evolutionary,stanley1973ecological,bonner1988evolution}.
Indeed, such effects arise in experiments that introduce known microbial predators to populations of naive prey \citep{boraas1998phagotrophy,yoshida2003rapid,herron2019denovo,pentz2015predator}.

Leveraging a digital model system to study synthetic instances of multicell evolution \textit{in silico}, we observe spontaneous niche construction effects and test their relationship to evolved complexity.
Model digital organisms are controlled by heritable genetic programs and act analogously to biological cells: replicating, mutating, competing, and thus evolving \unskip\citep{pennock2007models}.
Though artificial by nature, such tractable models can nonetheless illuminate principles general to biological life and beyond \citep{adami2000evolution,lenski2003evolutionary,zaman2014coevolution}.
For instance, niche construction and Evolutionary Transitions in Individuality (ETI) --- such as multicellularity --- have guided work among computer scientists to realize the ``open-ended'' creativity of evolution in abiotic systems \citep{dolson2019constructive,soros2014identifying,huizinga2018emergence,lehman2012beyond,nguyen2015,stanley2019open,packard2019overview,taylor2016open}.

The next section describes our experimental system, detailing how model digital organisms replicate, form multicells, and mediate interactions between them.
Following this, we examine the history of a case study lineage, showing how genotype complexity, phenotype complexity, and morphological novelty can arise in early multicell evolution with much greater detail than previously possible.
Competition assays reveal that our case study lineage became fit when abundant --- in other words, when shaping its own environmental context.
We next test whether this niche construction effect exerted self-acting biotic selection.

We find it does, and evolutionary replay experiments further show this self-acting biotic selection to lock in evolved complexity.
Finally, we conclude by examining the broader association between niche construction and complexity across many independent populations in our model system.
