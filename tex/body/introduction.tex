\section{Introduction}

Why should complex multicellular organisms exist, when simpler unicellular organisms grow biomass with substantially greater efficiency \citep{lynch2024bioenergetic}?
Though somatic division of labor and economies of scale can produce some synergistic benefit \citep{corning2015synergistic,niklas2014evolutionary}, such adaptation --- being outweighed by structural overheads and accumulated metabolic impedimenta --- cannot explain complex multicellularity.
Instead, some argue complex multicellularity to primarily reflect neutral processes \citep{TODO}, arising by gradual diffusion from a lower complexity bound \citep{gould1990wonderful}.
As highlighted by the drift-barrier hypothesis, modest population sizes among multicells should indeed act to accentuate evolutionary drift \citep{lynch2003origins}.
To compound this stochasticity, epistatic lock-in of arbitrary contingencies may act as a ratchet for complexity accumulation \citep{stoltzfus1999possibility,muozgmez2021constructive}.

An alternate adaptive explanation, however, may complement the neutralist framing: niche construction, where feedback from organisms' evolved traits shapes their own environmental context \citep{oldingsmee2003niche,odlingsmee1988niche,levins1985dialectical}.
Where complex traits become advantaged by such niche construction effects, they may be selected for \citep{taylor2004niche,waddington2008paradigm,laland2014niche,creanza2016cultural}.
In the context of multicellularity, for instance, large body size may be selected as a means to resist predation \citep{niklas2014evolutionary,stanley1973ecological,bonner1988evolution}.
Indeed, such effects arise in experiments that introduce microbial predators to populations of naive prey \citep{boraas1998phagotrophy,yoshida2003rapid,herron2019denovo,pentz2015predator}.

To date, however, experimental approaches have yet to demonstrate \textit{de novo} niche construction during early multicell evolution, and test its connection to evolved complexity \citep{goldsby2012task,goldsby2014evolutionary,bozdag2023denovo,ratcliff2012experimental,herron2016origins,kalambokidis2023multispecies}.

Leveraging a digital model system to study synthetic instances of multicell evolution \textit{in silico}, we observe a case of spontaneous niche construction that stabilizes substantially elevated genetic and phenotypic complexity.
% ^ and it's surprising that... _____ some tension that we resolve
Furthermore, with much greater detail than previously possible, we report how evolution of complexity unfolds in this case; surprisingly, we find the evolved strain to effectively impose selection to maintain complexity upon itself.
Critical to the work's tractability is our use of digital organisms as a model system: computer programs that replicate, mutate, compete, and thus evolve --- analogous to genomes within biological cells \citep{pennock2007models}.
Although important limitations arise in using an artificial substrate \citep{lynch2025complexity}, such an approach can nonetheless illuminate principles that generalize any evolving system \citep{adami2000evolution,lenski2003evolutionary,zaman2014coevolution}.
Indeed, among computer scientists, niche construction and Evolutionary Transitions in Individuality (ETI) --- such as multicellularity --- have been highlighted in efforts to engineer artificial ``open-ended'' creativity \citep{dolson2019constructive,soros2014identifying,huizinga2018emergence,lehman2012beyond,nguyen2015,stanley2019open,packard2019overview,taylor2016open}.

The next section describes our experimental system, detailing how model digital organisms replicate, form multicells, and interact with other multicells.
Following this, we examine complexity, novelty, and adaptation across the genomic and phenotypic history of a case study lineage exhibiting high genetic complexity, high phenotypic complexity, and morphological innovation.
Further examining this high-complexity lineage, competition assays reveal it evolved to become fit when abundant --- in other words, when serving as its own environmental context.
Using further competition assays, we next test whether this niche construction effect substantially drive selection on the high-complexity lineage.
