\caption{%
\textbf{Co-evolutionary adaptation creates genotype complexity.}
\footnotesize
In round-100 isolate of case study background strain, 17 genome sites exhibited adaptive benefit compared to single-site knockouts when tested with case study strain absent.
However, with case study strain present, adaptive benefit was detected at an additional 15 genome sites.
Co-evolutionary adaptation to case study strain thus accounts for 47\% (15/32) of detected genotype complexity in background strain (panel \ref{fig:ecogwas:sites}).
To assess whether co-evolutionary adaptation contributed to genotype complexity more generally, we screened more broadly for adaptive sites specific to co-evolved context --- and tested whether these sites associated with overall co-evolutionary adaptation.
Among second-round replicates ($n=400$), strains with weak adaptation to co-evolved context (fewer than 10\% competition-outcome flips and less than 5\% net change in ``measuring stick'' fitness  vs. no-background competitions) were selected for knockout analysis ($n=7$).
As comparison, $n=7$ strains with strongest adaptation to co-evolved context were also subjected to knockout analysis.
To ensure sign-change sensitivity, both samples were restricted to strains with moderate absolute fitness (i.e., no-background ``measuring stick'' fitness between 0.25 and 0.75).
Scatterplots pool single-site knockout fitness outcomes across sample strains, with dashed region designated adaptation specific to co-evolved background (Panel \ref{fig:ecogwas:ythresh045}, left).
Here, fitness $>0.5$ indicates advantage over wildtype.
Strains with strong adaptation to co-evolved context exhibit greater added genetic complexity in that context (Mann-Whitney test, $p=0.016$, Panel \ref{fig:ecogwas:ythresh045}, right).
This relationship remains consistent under more stringent criteria for neutral fitness (threshold 0.5, vs. 0.45 here; Mann-Whitney test, $p=0.013$, Cliff's delta 0.78; Supplementary Figure \ref{fig:ecogwas-supp}).
