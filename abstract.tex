\begin{abstract}
Complex multicellularity exemplifies the surprising constructive power of biological evolution.

While natural history reflects extensive innovation in multicellular form and function, explanations why such complexity should evolve remain unsettled.
To test this question experimentally, we reproduced early multicell evolution using a digital organism model: assemblages of cell-like computer programs that collectively replicate, mutate, and compete.

Among evolutionary outcomes, we observed an extreme outlier in genetic and phenotypic complexity measures exhibiting patterned multicellular growth and multi-stage life history.
Case study analysis of this lineage revealed that it had reshaped environmental context, by imposing collective crowding interference on alternate morphologies.
In replay experiments, we found that this strain's complex traits, in fact, were maintained by its biotic selection.
That is, emergent eco-evolutionary feedback locked in complex multicellularity.
Similar patterns arose broadly across evolutionary trials.
Surprisingly, we found no substantial association between complexity and general multicell fitness --- instead, complexity consistently associated with signatures of biotic selection.
Investigating further, we find that complex traits arose not only in self-constructed niches, but also under selection by co-evolving ecological partners.
These findings realize a long-theorized mechanism thought to drive complex multicellularity: biotic selection as an eco-evolutionary complexity ratchet, laying bare a fundamental ingredient of biological life's endless forms.
\end{abstract}
